Automotive Communication Display Console

Capstone / Mongo Ventures

Overview

The Automotive Communication Display Console, or ACDC, was my senior design capstone project, sponsored by Brian Gladden of Mongo Ventures. The idea started from a simple problem: drivers share the road but don't have a good way to communicate with each other. Horns, headlights, and hand gestures only get you so far. They can't convey anything nuanced, like a flat tire or a thank you, and that ambiguity leads to unsafe conditions and frustration on the road. Our solution was an externally mounted, self-powered display that lets drivers send short messages to the vehicles around them, controlled through a smartphone app rather than any in-car interaction, since the last thing we wanted was to add another distraction behind the wheel.

My Role

As the only person in the group with previous mechanical experience and CAD work, it was decided that my role would be the mechanical design. This entailed the enclosure that everything would go into, how our device mounts to the vehicle, and maintaining waterproofing of our components, among other things. Though my design was mechanical, that didn't mean I worked purely on the mechanical side. Capstone is about working as a team, which meant I had to design around the components my groupmates wanted to use and adapt to that.

The Redesign

Halfway through the project, the displays we'd designed around ended up not working, mostly because they didn't hold up to the specs they were advertised with, and didn't work with our electronics. Those displays were IP65 rated, which was the level of waterproofing we wanted to achieve. With that setup, my solution was to mount them externally, since that meant very few places I'd need to waterproof on the enclosure. Once those displays were out, we had to switch to a new set that weren't waterproof at all, which meant they now had to be mounted internally with viewing windows cut into the enclosure. That was a much harder problem to waterproof, and it meant redoing the entire enclosure design from the ground up.

What I Learned

I'll be honest, at first I didn't like this project. I was a computer engineer doing a job that felt like it should've gone to a mechanical engineer. But looking back, it taught me more than just mechanical design. It was nice to go back and relearn CAD, but more importantly, it taught me how to adapt and use skills my degree itself didn't teach me: risk analysis, and how to comfortably iterate through a project. To test early, expect failure, and adjust rather than treat a design as final. To not go with the first idea out of your head. This was the first real project I worked on with actual constraints, where my designs had to be sufficiently tested, because no one wants a 45 lb box flying off the roof of their car. This project ended up being genuinely enjoyable, and it made me realize that being a computer engineer doesn't mean my skills look like every other computer engineer's. The mechanical design side is still something I really enjoy.
